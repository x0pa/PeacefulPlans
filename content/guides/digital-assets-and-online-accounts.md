So much of a life now arrives without paper. Bank statements land in an inbox. Photographs live in a cloud account rather than in an album. A family business may keep its records, its storefront and its income inside online services that no one else in the family has ever opened. When a person dies, all of that still has to be found, understood and dealt with, usually by an executor who begins with no map at all. This guide explains what the law in Canada says about that access, where the law is still unclear, and what a calm plan looks like while you are here to make one. It is general legal information only, not legal advice for your own situation. For your own circumstances, speak with a lawyer in your province, or a notary in Quebec.

Digital assets belong inside a complete will, not beside it. Our guide to [what to put in a will](/guides/what-to-put-in-a-will/) places them in their full context, beside the executor, the gifts and the residue, and our guide to [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor/) covers the conversation to have with the person who would carry out this part of the work as well.

## What counts as a digital asset?

Start with the definition the law reformers chose. The Uniform Law Conference of Canada adopted the Uniform Access to Digital Assets by Fiduciaries Act in 2016, and it defines a digital asset as a record that is created, recorded, transmitted or stored in digital or other intangible form by electronic, magnetic or optical means or by any other similar means. The working group that wrote the Act explained that the term reaches any type of electronically stored information, including information stored on a computer and on other digital devices, content uploaded onto websites ranging from photos to documents, and rights in digital property such as domain names or digital entitlements associated with online games and material created online.

New Brunswick law reform notes give the same idea in the language of an ordinary household. Nearly everyone today has digital assets, and the examples offered there include electronic information about bank accounts, securities and insurance policies, email accounts, virtual currency, audio and video files, online music, electronic books, gaming credits, loyalty points, blogs, social media posts and family photographs, whether those things are stored in the cloud or on a personal computer, a laptop, a tablet or another storage device.

One distinction in the uniform Act deserves plain statement, because it prevents a common misunderstanding. Access to a record is not the same as ownership of the money the record describes. A bank account holds funds that pass under the ordinary rules of your estate. The online record is the evidence of that account and the doorway to it. The uniform Act deals with the doorway. It reinforces the right of a fiduciary to access online records in the same way that no one would refuse that fiduciary access to paper records, without changing who owns the funds themselves.

It also helps to sort your own digital life into two broad groups as you read. Some assets carry money or legal rights, such as a domain name, a cryptocurrency wallet, an online store, a loyalty balance or work you created and published online. Others carry memory and meaning, such as photographs, messages, journals and social accounts. An executor needs a plan for both groups, and the plan for each group will often point in a different direction.

## What does the law let your executor touch?

The word the uniform Act uses is fiduciary, and it covers four roles. A fiduciary can be a personal representative for a person who has died, a guardian appointed for an account holder, an attorney acting under a power of attorney, or a trustee appointed to hold property in trust. Under the uniform model, a personal representative has access to all of the digital assets of the person who died unless that person expressly indicated otherwise in a will or in another document. An attorney acting under a power of attorney has access on a similar basis, a trustee may access a digital asset held by the trust, and a guardian acts in accordance with a court order. The duties that govern fiduciaries for ordinary property apply to digital assets as well.

Only some Canadian jurisdictions have turned that model into their own statute so far. Saskatchewan led with The Fiduciaries Access to Digital Information Act, which came into force on June 29, 2020. Prince Edward Island followed with its Access to Digital Assets Act, proclaimed on January 1, 2022. New Brunswick and Yukon have since enacted their own versions based on the same model. In those places, the starting point is clear. A fiduciary has a right of access, that right follows the instructions in a will and in the other governing documents, and a custodian that holds the asset has a duty to respond.

In the rest of Canada, the honest answer is that the law remains unsettled. No comprehensive national statute governs access to the digital assets of a person who has died, and in a province without its own statute an executor may meet a service provider that relies on its service agreement, on privacy law, or on its own internal process for deaths. Research funded through the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada reached a similar picture from the consumer side. The policies of businesses that host data generally say that an account will remain active as long as the business has not been notified of the death according to its own procedures, only some businesses provide a way for a person to record what should happen to data after death, and most keep significant discretion over requests for deletion or access from loved ones. The same research describes the law that applies to digital death as an area full of uncertainties, in which Canadian laws greatly restrict access to the personal information of a person who has died.

Service agreements matter inside that uncertainty. The uniform model treats a clause that limits fiduciary access as unenforceable unless the account holder agreed to that limit by a separate affirmative act, apart from agreeing to the rest of the service agreement. Outside the provinces that have enacted the model, a clause buried in terms you accepted years ago may still shape what a provider will do, and a provider based outside Canada may apply its home rules as well. None of this is a reason for despair. It is a reason to write your wishes down expressly, because a clear instruction in a will is the strongest tool an executor has in any province, and in the enacted provinces it is the document the statute itself tells everyone to follow.

The same thinking applies while you are alive but unable to act. The documents that speak in that season are your power of attorney for property and your document for personal care or health decisions, and our guide to [power of attorney in Canada](/guides/power-of-attorney-in-canada-two-documents/) explains that pair. If you want the person you name there to be able to reach an online account in order to pay a bill or protect a business, say so in that document as well rather than assuming the authority carries over on its own.

## Should you put passwords in your will?

No, and the reasons are practical rather than fussy. A will that goes through probate can become part of a court file, and a court file is the wrong home for the keys to your financial life. Passwords also change. A will should be reviewed every few years, while a password may change every few months, and a will that lists credentials will be out of date long before it is read. Worst of all, a list of passwords inside a will gives a reader everything at once, with no control over who that reader turns out to be.

The calmer design separates the instruction from the key. The will itself should name your executor, give that executor express authority to access, manage, copy, transfer and close your digital assets, and record any strong wishes you hold, such as photographs to be saved for your children or an account you want deleted outright. The keys live elsewhere, in a password manager that your executor knows how to reach, or in another secure place you have chosen with care. A separate inventory, kept with your documents or shared through the password manager, then connects the two. It names each account, says what you want done with it, and points to where access lives, without printing a single password on paper that anyone can pick up.

Where a service offers its own setting for what happens after a death, use it as well. Some large providers let you name a person who may act for the account, or choose in advance whether data should be deleted after a period without activity. Those settings speak directly to the provider at the moment your executor most needs to be heard, and they cost only a few minutes while you are well.

## Building the inventory your executor will actually use

An inventory earns its keep by being findable, current and plain. For each account, record the name of the service, the user name or email address tied to it, what the account is for, and what you want done with it when you die. The choices are usually simple to state. Save and share the contents with family. Transfer the asset or the balance where the terms allow it. Download what matters and then close the account. Close the account and delete the contents. Turn a social account into a memorial if the service offers that path and if that is what you would want.

Add the details an executor hunts for at two in the morning. Note any subscription that charges a card on a regular cycle, so it can be stopped before it drains an estate account for months. Note where the recovery phrase for a cryptocurrency wallet lives, described by its location only, and note the wallet type and the exchange accounts you use. Note renewal dates for domain names, because a lapsed domain can take a family business or a long running blog with it. Note where family photographs truly live, which may be across several phones and several cloud accounts, and say which collections matter most if choices have to be made quickly. Our [documents checklist](/tools/documents-checklist/) gives this inventory a home beside the papers an executor needs for the rest of the estate, and our [readiness checklist](/tools/readiness-checklist/) pulls the whole plan into one calm review.

Keep the inventory current in the same rhythm as the will itself. Accounts open and close constantly. A short review once a year, perhaps when you renew a password or file a tax return, keeps the list honest without turning it into a second job.

## Money and rights that only exist online

Some digital assets need extra care because the value is real and the path to it is fragile. Cryptocurrency is the clearest example. The Canada Revenue Agency explains that a cryptocurrency is a type of virtual asset protected by cryptography, that it usually uses a system called a blockchain to record and keep a history of transactions, and that income from transactions involving cryptocurrency is treated for income tax purposes as business income or as a capital gain depending on the circumstances. Where cryptocurrency is held as capital property, the Agency says you must record and track the cost when you acquired it so that a gain can be reported accurately when you sell it. An executor inherits that record keeping duty for the estate, and can only meet it if your records of purchases, sales and wallet locations survive you.

The harder truth sits underneath the tax point. A cryptocurrency wallet is reached by keys, and keys that no one can find cannot be recreated by a court order. If you hold cryptocurrency in a wallet you control yourself, your plan must include a secure way for your executor to learn where the means of access is kept. That way should never be the recovery phrase written in the will or in the general inventory. It should be a separate secure location, named only by where it is, and shared in a manner you would still be comfortable with if the inventory itself were read by a stranger.

Domain names and loyalty balances sit at the other end of the same lesson. A domain name is a right that renews on a schedule and can be transferred if the registrar account can be reached in time. Loyalty points and program balances are entitlements governed by the terms of the program you joined, and programs differ widely on whether a balance can move to an estate or to a spouse, and on how quickly a balance expires after a death. Because those terms are set by each program and can change, check them while you can still ask the program directly, and record the answer in your inventory rather than leaving your family to argue from memory.

## Quebec settles digital assets under its own system

Quebec readers should not stretch the descriptions above over their own estates. Quebec settles a succession under the Civil Code of Quebec, and the person who settles the estate is a liquidator rather than an executor. Gouvernement du Québec advises naming a liquidator in your will and naming a replacement in case that person dies before you or cannot or will not accept, because without an appointment your heirs must settle the estate themselves or agree on a liquidator, and a court may have to appoint one if they cannot agree. The liquidator gathers the property of the succession, and in a modern estate that property includes records and rights that exist only in online accounts.

Quebec has its own legal framework for documents in electronic form, and it has not simply adopted the uniform Act as a Quebec statute in the material reviewed for this guide. Access for a liquidator therefore rests on the Civil Code, on the instructions in the will, and on the service agreements and privacy rules that apply to each account, rather than on a single digital assets statute of the Saskatchewan or Prince Edward Island kind. That makes an express clause in a Quebec will, and a clear inventory kept with it, even more important rather than less. A separate guide in this series will cover wills in Quebec on their own terms, including the notarial will, and Quebec readers should treat that guide and the Quebec sources listed below as their starting point.

## Keeping the plan current

A digital plan ages faster than any other part of an estate plan, because accounts change even when a family does not. Review the inventory and the digital clause in your will after the same moments that call for a will review in general, a marriage or a separation, a birth, a move to another province, the start or sale of a business, or the death of the person you named as executor. Add new accounts as you open them rather than trusting yourself to remember a grand cleanup later. Tell your executor that the inventory exists and where it lives, and tell them again if you move it. A plan no one can find is only a good intention.

## The calm takeaway

Your online life is part of your estate, whether your will mentions it or not. What a will can do is turn a locked door into a set of clear instructions, an executor with express authority, an inventory that names what exists and what should happen to it, and keys kept safely apart from the document itself. In the provinces that have enacted the uniform model, that paperwork connects to a statute built for exactly this work. Everywhere in Canada, it spares your family guesswork at the moment they can least afford it. Write the plan down while the accounts, and the choices, are still yours.
