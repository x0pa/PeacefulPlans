Ask a room of Canadians whether they have a power of attorney and most will answer as if there were one document with that name. In most of Canada there are two, and they do entirely different work. One looks after your money and property if you cannot. The other speaks for your care and health when you cannot speak for yourself. Confusing them is easy, common, and worth clearing up, because a family that discovers the gap during a health crisis has run out of calm options. This guide sets the two side by side.

## The money document, power of attorney for property

A power of attorney for property names a person, called your attorney in this legal sense, though they need not be a lawyer, to handle financial matters on your behalf. Depending on how the document is written, that can include paying bills, banking, managing investments, collecting income, and dealing with property. It can be broad or narrow, immediate or designed to begin only when you become incapable, and in every case it ends at your death. After death, the will and the executor take over, and the attorney's authority simply stops.

This document is about incapacity far more than about convenience. Strokes, accidents, and dementia do not ask whether your mortgage is due. Without a power of attorney for property, no one, not even a spouse, automatically gains the right to manage your accounts or sell your house. A family in that position usually has to apply to a court or a public guardian office for authority, a slow and public process at the worst possible time.

## The care document, decisions about you

The second document covers personal care and health decisions: where you live, what care you receive, and consent to treatment when you cannot give it yourself. The person you name may be asked to weigh treatments, speak with doctors, and apply the wishes you expressed while you were able. It is the more intimate of the two documents, and the person for the job is the person who knows your values and can hold them steady under pressure in a hospital corridor.

You may name the same person for both documents, and many spouses do exactly that. You may also split them: a methodical daughter for the money, a steadfast son for the care. Both choices are normal. What matters is that each role is filled by someone suited to it, who knows they have been named.

## The names change at the provincial border

Here is where Canadians get tripped, because the care document wears a different name in much of the country. In Ontario it is the Power of Attorney for Personal Care. In Alberta the care document is a Personal Directive, and the person you name is called your agent; the money document in Alberta is an Enduring Power of Attorney. In British Columbia the care and health document is a Representation Agreement, naming a representative, while the money side is again an Enduring Power of Attorney. In Manitoba you make an enduring power of attorney for property, and a separate Health Care Directive that can name a proxy to make care decisions for you.

The pattern underneath the names is consistent even where the vocabulary is not: one instrument for money, one for care, each governed by the law of your province. If you move provinces, have both documents reviewed, because a document drafted under one province's law may not behave as you expect under another's.

Quebec, as ever, follows its own road. The Quebec instrument is the Protection Mandate, prepared in advance and naming a mandatary. It is a genuine difference in structure, not merely in name: one mandate can cover both your property and your person, and it generally takes effect only after a court process, called homologation, confirms your incapacity. Nothing about the two document pattern in the rest of this guide should be assumed to describe Quebec.

## Enduring and continuing, the words that make it work

A plain power of attorney, of the ordinary kind once used for a single task, can end the moment you lose capacity, which is precisely when your family needs it most. The documents in this guide are written to survive that moment. Ontario calls its money document a Continuing Power of Attorney for Property. Other provinces use the word enduring to mean the same thing: the authority continues through incapacity because the document says so, in the way that province's law requires.

Read for that word, or have a lawyer confirm it is there. A power of attorney that quietly expires on incapacity is a plan that fails at the exact moment it was designed for, and families rarely discover the problem in advance.

## What these documents cannot do

Neither document lets your attorney change your will, make a new will for you, or give your property away as they please. An attorney must act in your interests, keep records, and stay within the authority you granted. Neither document survives your death, and neither replaces a will; the two sets of papers work in sequence, the powers of attorney covering your lifetime and the will beginning where they end. Knowing the boundary protects the person you name as much as it protects you, because it tells them where their authority stops.

## Getting both in place

Both documents are provincial, both must follow your province's signing and witnessing rules, and both are available through lawyers, through some online services, and in several provinces through official government forms. The signing rules are strict and differ by province, so follow them exactly. Then store the documents where they can be reached: your attorney should know where the originals are, your bank may want to see the property document in advance, and a copy of the care document belongs with your health information and, in provinces that offer one, in the registry your province provides.

## The calm takeaway

Two documents, two jobs: one guards your money, one guards your care, and both speak only while you live and cannot speak for yourself. Learn the names your own province uses, sign both while the signing is easy, and tell the people you named. It is an afternoon of paperwork that can spare your family a courtroom.
