Naming an executor is usually treated as an honour to bestow. It is more honest to treat it as a job to fill, because that is what it becomes: a stretch of paperwork, phone calls, deadlines, and other people's feelings, undertaken in the middle of grief. The kindest thing you can do for the person you name is to choose them well, ask them properly, and leave the job organized. This guide covers what the work involves, who tends to do it well, and the conversation to have long before the will is ever opened.

## What the job actually involves

In the first days, the executor helps with funeral arrangements and secures the home and belongings. In the first months, they gather the assets, notify banks, insurers, pension plans, and government programs, and apply to the court for probate where the estate requires it. They arrange valuations, keep careful records, and make sure the deceased's debts are identified and paid in the right order.

Then comes the part nobody pictures. The executor files the final tax return, and often further returns besides, and waits for a clearance certificate from the Canada Revenue Agency before distributing the estate in full. They deal with the house, whether that means selling it or transferring it. Only at the end do they divide what remains among the beneficiaries, exactly as the will directs, and account to those beneficiaries for every dollar. In a settled, simple estate this takes a year or so. In a complicated one, it takes longer. It is real work, and it deserves to be chosen like work.

## The qualities that matter

Trustworthiness stands first, because the executor handles everything you own with very little day to day supervision. Organization stands close behind: the job rewards someone who keeps records, answers letters, and does not lose forms. Availability matters more than affection. An executor who lives nearby can lock a house, meet a banker, and sign in person. An executor across the country can still serve, and many do, but distance adds friction to every step, and some provinces add rules about out of province executors as well.

Age and stage of life deserve an honest look. Naming a parent is natural when you are young, and less wise as the years pass and the odds shift. Naming all your children together sounds fair and often works, but it can also turn every decision into a meeting. One capable child, clearly named, with siblings who understand why, frequently serves an estate better than a committee of equals. What matters most is temperament: the executor will deliver news, hold boundaries, and sometimes say no to people they love. Choose someone who can do that gently and still do it.

## Family, fairness, and friction

Executors are usually family, and usually they should be. Just notice where the friction lives before you decide. If one child is receiving more than another, for reasons that are fair and still tender, asking the other child to administer that gift is asking a great deal. If two branches of a blended family barely speak, naming one side to settle the estate hands them authority over the other's inheritance, and even scrupulous fairness may not be believed.

There are middle paths. Two executors acting together, one from each side, can balance a family. A trust company or a professional can serve where the estate is large, the family is strained, or no natural candidate exists, in exchange for a fee paid from the estate. Naming a professional is not a failure of family. Sometimes it is the gift that lets family members simply grieve.

## Always name a backup

Executors are mortal too. The person you name may die before you, move away, fall ill, or simply reach a season of life where the job is too much, and an executor is allowed to decline when the time comes. If your will names no one else, the family is back to a court application, choosing a replacement under stress.

A backup executor, sometimes called a substitute or alternate, costs you one sentence in the will and saves all of that. Many people name a spouse first and an adult child or trusted friend as backup. Review the pairing whenever you review the will itself, because the right backup at forty may be the wrong one at seventy.

## The conversation itself

Ask before you write the name down. The conversation does not need to be solemn, and it does not need to share the contents of the will. It needs to cover four things. You are making your will, and you would like them to serve as executor. The job involves settling accounts, taxes, and the house, over about a year. They are free to say no, now or later, and you would rather hear it now. And there is a backup named after them, so they are not the only line of defence.

Then listen. Some people will be honoured and steady. Some will hesitate, and the hesitation is information, not ingratitude. A reluctant executor who agrees out of guilt serves nobody, least of all your beneficiaries.

## Where the will lives

An executor who cannot find the will cannot use it, and this small practical point defeats more estates than any legal subtlety. Tell your executor where the original is kept, whether that is a lawyer's office, a safety deposit box, a home safe, or a provincial wills registry where your province offers one. Quebec readers will know the estate representative there is called a liquidator, and the same advice holds: the person named needs to know the document exists and where it waits. Leave a note with your important papers listing accounts, insurers, and advisors. It is not part of the will. It is the map that lets the will work.

## The calm takeaway

Choose your executor the way you would hire for a year of careful, sensitive work, because that is the job. Ask them while you can still hear the answer, name a backup behind them, and leave the papers somewhere findable. Done well, this is one of the most considerate pages in your whole plan.
