There is more than one honest way to make a valid will in Canada, and the best route depends on how complicated your life is, not on how much you can spend. A simple estate in the hands of a careful person can produce a perfectly good will without a law office. A complicated estate can defeat even a good template. This guide walks through the four routes most Canadians actually use, with the strengths and limits of each stated plainly.

## Route one, a lawyer

A lawyer who practises in wills and estates interviews you, drafts the document, supervises its signing, and usually stores a copy or a note of where the original lives. You pay for judgment as much as for paper. A good lawyer asks the questions a form cannot: What happens if your executor dies before you? If a beneficiary dies before you? If your daughter's marriage ends? Who takes the cottage, and who pays its costs?

This route costs the most, generally several hundred dollars for a straightforward will and more where trusts or business interests are involved. It is also the route least likely to produce a document that fails when it is needed, which is the only test that matters. For many families, one visit in a decade is the whole expense.

## Route two, an online will service

Online services walk you through questions and generate a will from your answers, which you then print and sign with witnesses. The better services are built with Canadian lawyers, ask about your province, and prompt you on executors, guardians, and backup plans. They cost far less than a law office and far more than nothing, and for a simple, settled situation they can produce a sound document.

Their limit is the limit of every questionnaire. A service cannot notice the thing you did not mention. It will not pause over the fact that your son lives in another country, that your RRSP beneficiary form contradicts your will, or that leaving the house to one child and love to the others tends to end in court. If your situation has any moving parts, treat an online draft as a starting point for a lawyer's review, not as the finished work.

## Route three, a holograph will

A holograph will is written entirely in your own handwriting and signed by you, with no typed parts and, in the provinces that accept it, no witnesses required. Most provinces accept holograph wills, including Ontario, Alberta, Manitoba, and Saskatchewan. Quebec accepts a will written entirely by hand as well, though it must generally be proven after death in a way a Quebec notarial will does not. British Columbia is the notable exception in the other direction: a purely handwritten will is not automatically valid there, and a court has only limited power to rescue a document that falls short of the usual rules.

The appeal is obvious. It costs nothing and can be done tonight. The risks are just as real. Handwritten wills are frequently unclear, and unclear is expensive. They omit executors' backup plans, trusts for young beneficiaries, and residue clauses, the clause that catches everything you forgot to mention. They get lost, because nobody knows where they are. A holograph will is a genuine legal tool and a reasonable bridge in an urgent moment. It is rarely the best finished plan.

## Route four, a printed kit or a form

Stationery store kits and free templates sit between the online service and the holograph page. They provide structure: spaces for an executor, for beneficiaries, for guardians. They provide no guidance at all, and their one size language sometimes belongs to another province or another country entirely. If you use one, confirm that it speaks to your province's law, fill in every section including the residue, and follow the signing rules below with extra care, because no one will check your work but the court, later.

## Witnessing, the part people get wrong

Most wills that fail, fail at the signing, not the writing. In most provinces, a typed or printed will must be signed by you in the presence of two witnesses, who watch you sign and then sign themselves in your presence and in each other's. Neither witness should be a beneficiary or the spouse of a beneficiary. In many provinces, a gift to a witness can be struck out even where the rest of the will survives, which is a painful way to learn the rule.

Choose witnesses who are adults, easy to find later, and connected to nothing in the document. Neighbours and coworkers serve well. Some provinces now allow signing by video call under set conditions, a change that began during the pandemic years, but the conditions are specific, and a kitchen table with two people on it remains the safest room in the country for this job. Quebec, again, is its own world: the notarial will, signed before a notary and one witness, is the form most Quebecers use, and it carries advantages no other route in Canada quite matches.

## When a lawyer is genuinely worth it

Some situations repay professional help many times over. A blended family, where fairness has more than one definition. A child or beneficiary with a disability, where a badly structured gift can interrupt benefits. A business, a farm, or property in another province or country. A large estate, a strained family, or any wish to leave someone out who would expect to be in. In each case the will is not really a form. It is a piece of engineering, and it will be load tested by grief.

## The calm takeaway

For a simple estate, any careful route can work, and a finished modest will beats a perfect one that exists only as an intention. For a complicated estate, the lawyer's fee is not the cost of the document. It is the cost of the document working. Choose the route that matches your life, sign it properly, and tell someone where it is.
