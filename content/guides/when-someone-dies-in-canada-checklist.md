## Before the list, one breath

If you are reading this because someone has just died, this page was written for exactly the hour you are in. Nothing here needs to happen all at once. The steps are gathered in the order they usually arise, day, week, and month, and nearly everything after the first day can wait until you have slept, eaten, and had help arrive. Take the list one line at a time, and let other people carry as much of it as they offer to carry.

## The first day

**Pronouncement and the first call.** When a death is expected, at home or in care, a doctor or nurse formally pronounces the death, and that pronouncement is what allows everything else to begin. If the death is unexpected, call emergency services first and follow their direction. In some situations the coroner or medical examiner becomes involved, which is routine and not a sign of anything wrong, and the body is released once their work is done.

**Call the funeral home.** If arrangements were planned ahead, use the funeral home your loved one chose. If not, call one you trust or one family recommends. The funeral home will arrange transfer of the body, whenever you are ready, and will guide nearly every step that follows. You do not need to have made a single decision about services before this call. Transfer first. Decisions later, at a table, with tea.

**Tell the closest people, and ask for help.** Call immediate family and one or two steady friends. Give someone the job of notifying wider circles so you do not repeat the hardest sentence fifty times. If your loved one belonged to a faith community, tell their leader, who may want to be present and who can help with what comes next.

**Secure the practical world.** Lock the home if it now sits empty. Care for pets. Move vehicles somewhere safe. Bring in mail and packages so the house does not advertise its emptiness. These small acts protect everything while the larger ones wait.

## The first week

**Register the death and get certificates.** The funeral home usually helps complete the statement of death and registers the death with the province. From that registration flow two documents families constantly confuse, so here is the plain version. The funeral home provides a proof of death document, sometimes called a funeral director statement of death, and banks, insurers, and many others accept it. The province issues the official death certificate, ordered from the provincial vital statistics office, and some organizations insist on that one. Ask the funeral home how many proofs they recommend. Order more copies of each than feels necessary. Chasing copies later, mid paperwork, is a miserable errand that a few extra dollars now prevents. Provincial names, fees, and forms differ, and Quebec runs its process through the Directeur de l’état civil, with documents in French, so follow your own province and its process.

**Find the will.** Look where your loved one kept important papers, and ask their lawyer, their bank, and family members. The will names the executor, the person legally responsible for settling the estate. If you are that person, the role begins now, gently. If no will exists, provincial law decides who may act, and a lawyer can explain the process where you live. Either way, nothing about the estate needs to be finished this week. It only needs to be started, and located.

**Hold the funeral or memorial.** With the funeral home, settle the form of the service, burial or cremation, and any wishes your loved one left in writing. Left wishes, in an envelope or a planning document, are a mercy here. Follow them, and let disagreements about taste be settled by the calm fact that these were their choices.

**Notify the inner circle of institutions.** Call the employer or pension administrators, and any organization that needs to know promptly. Arrange mail redirection if the home will stay empty. Keep a notebook, paper or phone, with every call logged, date, name of the person spoken to, and what was promised. In a fog of grief, the notebook remembers what you cannot.

## The first month

**Notify government, in a sensible order.** Service Canada should be told of the death so that payments stop correctly and survivor steps can begin. Old Age Security and Canada Pension Plan payments are not payable for the month after death, and any amounts received after that month generally must be returned, so prompt notice protects the estate from repayment confusion later. The Social Insurance Number is cancelled through the same process, which also quietly protects against identity misuse. The Canada Revenue Agency should be told as well, and their guidance for representatives of a deceased person explains exactly what they need and when.

**Notify banks, insurers, and creditors.** Banks will need the proof of death and, usually, the will, before releasing information, and the executor is the person they will deal with. Joint accounts and accounts with named beneficiaries often pass outside the estate entirely. Life insurance claims begin with a call to the insurer, who will send their forms. Credit card companies, lenders, and utilities should be told, accounts settled or transferred, and subscriptions stopped before they quietly bill another season.

**Apply for the benefits that exist for this moment.** Canada Pension Plan includes a death benefit, a single payment made to the estate or to an eligible applicant, and a survivor pension for an eligible spouse or common law partner, along with benefits that can help children of a deceased contributor. These exist, they matter, and many families miss them simply because nobody mentioned them in the first hard weeks. The amounts, the eligibility rules, and the application process itself are maintained in full, with current figures, on our sister site EveryBenefit, which tracks these programs closely and keeps them current. Go there, or to Service Canada directly, when you are ready to apply. What belongs on this page is only the pointer, said clearly and early. Do not let these applications slide past their deadlines while the paperwork feels heavy.

**Look after the estate, step by step.** Probate, the court process that confirms the will and the executor authority, is required in many situations and not in others, depending on the province and on what the deceased owned and how. A lawyer in the province where your loved one lived can say whether your estate needs it. Debts of the estate are paid from the estate, in an order the law sets, before what remains goes to the people named in the will. Keep every receipt. The executor accounts to the beneficiaries in the end, and orderly records turn that duty from a worry into a formality.

**File the final tax return, later in the month or the season.** A tax return is required for the year of death, and sometimes optional returns can help the estate. The deadlines are specific and they are not in the first weeks, so breathe. The Canada Revenue Agency publishes a calm, complete guide for exactly this task, and many executors hand it to an accountant in the end, which is a perfectly good decision.

## The calm takeaway

Day one is people and the funeral home. Week one is registration, certificates, the will, and the service. Month one is notifications, benefits, and the estate, in that order, with a notebook keeping track of all of it. Nothing on this list rewards rushing, and almost everything rewards being orderly and being kind to yourself while you do it.
