A will can look like a short document and still carry a whole life. The reason it works is structure. Almost every sound Canadian will answers the same set of questions in the same broad order: who does the work, what is owed, who receives what, who cares for the children, and what happens to everything that is left. This guide walks through each part in that order, so you can see what belongs in the document before you sit down to make one, by any of the routes described in our guide to [how to make a will in Canada](/guides/how-to-make-a-will-in-canada/).

Estate law, including wills, sits with the provinces and territories, as the Government of Canada notes on its estates and wills page. That means the shape of a will is shared across the country, while the signing rules and many details follow your own province. Quebec has its own system and its own section below.

## First, name the person who does the work

Every will needs an executor, the person or institution you name to gather your assets, pay what is owed, and distribute what remains according to your instructions. The Government of Canada guidance for older Canadians makes a practical point that is easy to skip: make sure the person or organization is willing to take on the job before you name them, and ask about fees if you are naming a professional such as a trust company. Being an executor takes time and involves a good deal of paperwork.

Name a backup as well. Your first choice may have died, moved away, or simply be unable to act when the moment comes, and a will with no one left to carry it out sends your family to court for a replacement. Our guide to [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor/) walks through the qualities that matter and the conversation to have before you write a name down.

## Start with a clear picture of what you own and what you owe

Before gifts come debts. In Ontario, for example, an estate trustee who acts under a will is responsible for winding up the affairs of the person who died, paying taxes, bills, and any other debts, collecting the estate assets, and only then distributing the residue to those entitled to it. British Columbia law speaks the same way, listing debts, funeral and testamentary expenses, and taxes among the payments an estate must handle. Most wills therefore direct the executor to settle lawful debts, taxes, and estate expenses early in the document, so nobody wonders which money is spoken for first.

You do not need to freeze a full inventory into the will itself, because accounts open, close, and change. What helps is preparing that inventory alongside the will, and keeping it current. Our [documents checklist](/tools/documents-checklist/) sets out the papers and details an executor needs to find, and a copy kept with your will gives your executor a map instead of a scavenger hunt.

## Specific gifts come next

This is the part most people picture when they picture a will. A specific gift leaves a named thing, or a set sum of money, to a named person or organization: the house to a spouse, a sum to each grandchild, a piece of jewellery to the niece who always loved it, a vehicle to a son. Describe each gift clearly enough that there is only one thing it can mean, and use full legal names for people, because families often share first names across generations.

For every major gift, answer one more question in the document: what happens if that person dies before you? Naming a backup beneficiary takes a line. Leaving the question open can send the gift into the residue, or into an argument. Clarity here is not fussiness. It is the whole point of writing things down.

## What is the residue clause, and why does it matter so much?

After debts, taxes, expenses, and specific gifts, whatever remains is called the residue of the estate. For many families the residue is most of the estate: bank accounts, investments that were never singled out, household contents, and anything acquired after the will was signed. The residue clause says who receives all of it, often in shares, such as equal shares to children, with backup names if one of them dies first.

This clause earns its keep quietly. British Columbia law states that property a will does not give away or otherwise dispose of must be distributed to the people who would be entitled if that property had no will behind it at all. In other words, whatever your will forgets, provincial intestacy rules remember, on their own terms. A complete residue clause, with backups, is what keeps every part of your estate inside your own plan. If you want to see what those default rules look like without a will, our guide on [dying without a will in Canada](/guides/do-you-need-a-will-in-canada/) lays them out.

## Who will raise your children, and who will look after their share?

If you have young children, a will is where you name a guardian for them, the person who would raise them if both parents were gone. Choose someone who knows your children, shares your values, and is willing in fact and not only in theory, and name a backup guardian as well. Talk to them first. A guardianship named in a will carries great weight, and it works best when it is not a surprise.

Money for those children needs its own thought. A will can set up a trust that holds a young beneficiary share, managed by a trustee you name, and used for the child care, health, and education until an age you choose, rather than handing a large sum over all at once at the age of majority. The guardian who raises the child and the trustee who holds the money can be the same person, but they do not have to be, and for some families separating the two jobs is the calmer design.

## Gifts to charity, care for a pet, and funeral wishes

A will can also carry the quieter wishes. You can leave a gift to a charity you have supported for years, using its full legal name. You can name the person who should take a pet, ideally someone who has already agreed, and some people leave that person a modest sum toward the animal care. You can record funeral and burial wishes as well, and many people do.

One practical caution belongs with funeral wishes. A will is sometimes first read closely after the funeral has already happened. If your funeral wishes matter to you, share them with your family directly as well, or record them with a funeral home, and treat the will as the backup copy rather than the only copy.

## What does not belong in your will?

Some things pass outside a will, under their own paperwork, and putting them in the will does not change that. The Canada Revenue Agency explains that who receives a TFSA after the holder dies can be determined by a designation in the TFSA contract, by the terms of the will, or by provincial succession law, and Quebec does not recognize some of these designations at all. Registered accounts and life insurance with a named beneficiary usually follow that beneficiary form, and jointly owned property usually passes to the surviving owner. Your will and your beneficiary forms need to agree with each other, so check both whenever you update either one.

A will also only speaks at death. The documents that speak while you are alive but unable to act are separate: a power of attorney for property and a document for personal care or health decisions, under names that vary by province. Our guide to [power of attorney in Canada](/guides/power-of-attorney-in-canada-two-documents/) explains the pair, and a complete plan holds both sets of papers, each in its own place.

## Quebec is its own section, on purpose

Quebec settles estates under the Civil Code of Quebec, and the vocabulary alone tells you the system is different. The person who settles the estate is a liquidator, not an executor. Gouvernement du Québec advises naming one in your will, and naming a replacement in case that person dies before you or cannot or will not accept, because without an appointment your heirs must settle the estate themselves or agree on a liquidator, and a court may have to appoint one if they cannot agree. A parent names a tutor for young children, rather than a guardian. The notarial will, signed before a notary, is an authentic act and does not have to be verified after death, while a holograph will and a will made before witnesses do have to be verified.

Because of those differences, the general descriptions in this guide should not be stretched over a Quebec estate without checking the Quebec rules directly. A separate guide in this series covers wills in Quebec on their own terms, built from Quebec sources, and Quebec readers should treat that guide, and the Quebec sources listed below, as their starting point.

## Signing, storing, and telling people where it is

What you put in a will only matters if the finished document is valid and findable. Sign it exactly the way your province requires, with the witnesses your province requires, following the steps in our guide to [how to make a will in Canada](/guides/how-to-make-a-will-in-canada/). Store the original somewhere safe and dry, and tell your executor where it is. A perfect will in an unknown drawer does your family no good at all, and a copy with a note about the original is a simple kindness. Our [readiness checklist](/tools/readiness-checklist/) pulls the storing and sharing steps together in one place.

## The calm takeaway

A complete will is not long because life is complicated. It is complete because it answers every question in order: an executor and a backup, debts and taxes settled, specific gifts with backups, guardians and trusts where children are involved, and a residue clause that leaves nothing to a default formula. Write those answers down while they are yours to give, and the document will do its work quietly, once, when it is needed.
