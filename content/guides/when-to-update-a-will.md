A will is a snapshot. It records your family, your property and your wishes on the day you signed it, and then life keeps moving. Children are born and grow up. Marriages begin and end. The people you trusted to carry out the plan grow older themselves. None of that movement updates the document on its own, and in some provinces the law quietly changes what the document does while it sits in the drawer. This guide walks through the moments that call for an update, the places where provincial law steps in without asking you, and the right way to make a change when one is needed. It is general legal information only, not legal advice for your own situation. For your own circumstances, speak with a lawyer in your province, or a notary in Quebec.

An update starts from a complete picture of what a will should hold. Our guide to [what to put in a will](/guides/what-to-put-in-a-will/) walks through the full contents, and our guide to [how to make a will in Canada](/guides/how-to-make-a-will-in-canada/) covers the signing rules that any replacement document must meet as well.

## How often should you review a will?

Build the habit before you need it. The Government of Canada advises older Canadians to review a will every five years, or sooner if there has been a change in the family, to make sure it continues to reflect their wishes. Five years is a long time in a household. In five years a child can be born and start school, a parent can die, a house can be bought and sold, and an executor can move across the country. Treat the five year mark as the longest a will should ever sit unopened, and treat every major life change in between as its own reason to sit down with the document.

A review is not the same as a rewrite. Many reviews end with the will left exactly as it is, because it still says what you mean. The value of the review is the certainty. You have checked that the people named are still alive, still willing and still the people you would choose, that the gifts still match the property you own, and that nothing in your family has changed the meaning of a single line. Our [readiness checklist](/tools/readiness-checklist/) pulls that review into one calm pass alongside the rest of your plan.

## Which life changes call for an update?

Start with family. A marriage is the loudest alarm, for reasons the next section explains in detail. The start of a common law relationship matters too, even though it revokes nothing on its own, because a partner who has built a life with you may have claims or expectations your old will never considered, and a will written while you were single will usually leave that partner nothing. A birth or an adoption calls for guardians to be named and for gifts to be rethought, and our guide to [guardians for minor children](/guides/guardians-for-minor-children/) covers that work in full. Children growing up changes the plan from the other direction. A will written for toddlers may hold money in trust until an age your children passed years ago, and a grown child may now be the natural choice as executor rather than the sibling you named in a different season.

Deaths inside the document call for updates as well. If a beneficiary dies before you, where does that gift go? Some wills answer the question and some leave it to chance. If your executor dies, moves away, falls ill or simply tells you the job no longer fits, the will needs a new name and a backup behind it, chosen with the care our guide to [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor/) describes. A falling out matters just as much as a death. A will that still names a person you no longer trust is a plan you no longer hold.

Property changes the arithmetic. Buying a home, selling one, starting or selling a business, receiving an inheritance or acquiring property in another province or another country can each bend an old plan out of shape. A gift of a house you no longer own is a gift of nothing. Equal shares among children can turn unequal when one asset grows to dominate the estate. And a move to another province deserves its own review even when nothing else has changed, because wills, marriage rules and spousal rights are provincial law in Canada, and the document you signed under one system will be read under another. Your digital life belongs in the same review, and our guide to [digital assets and online accounts](/guides/digital-assets-and-online-accounts/) explains how to fold accounts and online property into the plan.

## Does marriage revoke a will in Canada?

Here the honest answer is that Canada holds two opposite rules at once, and which one governs you depends on where you live. Manitoba keeps the older rule. The Province of Manitoba states that a will is almost always invalid if the person who made it marries after it is signed, in all but a few cases, and that it is essential to have a new will prepared after marriage. The exceptions are narrow ones, such as a will that declares it was made in contemplation of the marriage. A Manitoban who marries and does nothing will usually die with no will at all in the eyes of the law, however carefully the old one was written.

Ontario and British Columbia have moved the other way. In Ontario the provision of the Succession Law Reform Act that revoked a will on marriage was repealed effective January 1, 2022, so a marriage after that date leaves an earlier will standing. In British Columbia the Wills, Estates and Succession Act likewise provides that a will is not revoked by a change in circumstances, and marriage alone does not undo it. Notice what survival does not give you. A will that predates a marriage usually leaves the new spouse nothing, because that spouse was not part of the life of the testator when the gifts were chosen. The document survives and the plan fails, quietly, in the opposite direction from Manitoba. In every province, marriage is a reason to sit down with a lawyer and a fresh page.

One more warning belongs here. Entering a common law relationship revokes nothing anywhere in Canada, as Manitoba states expressly, and it changes no line of your will. Couples who build a long life together without marrying are the easiest people in the country for an old will to forget entirely.

## What do separation and divorce do to a will?

Divorce is where most provincial statutes step in, and they step in in a similar way. In Ontario, where a marriage is terminated by divorce or declared a nullity, gifts to the former spouse, the appointment of the former spouse as estate trustee and any power of appointment given to that spouse are revoked, and the will is read as if the former spouse had died before the testator, unless the will shows a contrary intention. Ontario has also extended that treatment to spouses who meet its definition of separated, in defined circumstances that include living separate and apart for three years or having a separation agreement or a court order in place. In British Columbia, section 56 of the Wills, Estates and Succession Act does the same work when spouses cease to be spouses under that Act. The gift, the appointment and the power are revoked, and the gift is distributed as if the spouse had died first, again subject to a contrary intention shown in the will.

Manitoba draws its line at a different place, and the difference is worth learning precisely. Manitoba reads a will as if a former spouse had died first where the maker has divorced, and it applies the same reading when a common law relationship has terminated within the meaning of its Wills Act. But Manitoba states plainly that wills are not interpreted this way when spouses merely separate, even if they have been separated for many years or are in divorce proceedings when one spouse dies. A separated spouse in Manitoba can still receive everything an old will leaves them. Separated spouses who want a different result must revise or revoke their wills themselves, because no statute will do it for them.

Read those rules together and the lesson is plain. The automatic protections are real, but they are patchwork. They differ by province, they turn on definitions of separation that a grieving family may have to argue about, they leave the rest of the will standing with gaps where a spouse used to be, and they say nothing about the new partner, the new children or the new wishes that arrived with the change. They are a safety net under a fall. They are not a plan. A new will written in your new circumstances is the plan.

## The papers that live outside your will

Updating the will is only half the job, because some of your most valuable property never passes through it. Beneficiary designations recorded with an issuer, on registered accounts, pension plans and insurance policies, speak for themselves. The Canada Revenue Agency explains that the type of beneficiary for a tax free savings account is determined by designations made in the account contract, by the terms of the will, or by the succession law of the province, and that a holder who wants to change a designation made in the past should contact the account issuer. That instruction generalises. After a marriage, a separation or a divorce, ask each institution who it currently has on file, and change the form with the institution where a change is needed, rather than assuming a new will has quietly rewritten every form you ever signed years ago at a bank desk.

Give the same attention to the documents that speak while you are alive. A separation or a new relationship is exactly the moment to reread your power of attorney and your care documents, because the person named there may be the very person your new will has just removed. Our guide to [power of attorney in Canada](/guides/power-of-attorney-in-canada-two-documents/) explains that pair and who each document empowers.

## How do you update a will properly?

Not with a pen on the signed document. Writing on a will, crossing out a name or adding a line in a margin is the one method to avoid entirely, because an alteration that was never properly executed can fail, and the marks can leave a family arguing over which version of you to believe. The lawful tools are two. The first is a codicil, a separate document of a testamentary nature that adds to or changes the will. The second is a new will, which replaces the old one entirely and is the cleaner path whenever the changes are more than minor.

Manitoba describes the choice in practical terms. If a person wants to make many changes, it may be easiest to prepare a new will, while a minor change, such as naming a new person to act as executor, can be done through a codicil. A codicil is not a shortcut around form. In Manitoba it must be witnessed in the same way as a will, by two or more people, unless it is entirely in the makers own handwriting and signed and dated by that person. Quebec gives the same warning in its own words. A codicil must meet the same conditions as the will in order to be valid, it must be read alongside the will it amends, and the Gouvernement du Québec notes that it is generally better to draw up a new will rather than amend an existing one, because readers will see what you wrote before alongside what you changed, and that pairing can lead to arguments.

Whichever tool you choose, finish the job completely. Sign and witness the new document exactly as your province requires. Tell your executor where the new original lives. Make sure the old document cannot be mistaken for the current one, because a drawer holding two signed wills and a codicil is an argument waiting for a date. ## Quebec updates a will under its own rules

Quebec readers should take the provincial sections above as a map of the rest of Canada, not as a description of home. Quebec successions run under the Civil Code of Québec, the person who settles the estate is a liquidator rather than an executor, and the vocabulary of gifts is the vocabulary of legacies. The Gouvernement du Québec confirms the starting point that matters most here. You can amend your will, or replace it entirely, as often as you want, so that it reflects your last wishes, for instance after a separation or a divorce, and only your most recent will is valid.

The mechanics carry Quebec signatures. A codicil in Quebec must meet the same conditions as the will it amends, and it may take a different form from the original, so a holograph codicil can amend a notarial will. If the will and the codicil were not drawn up before a notary, they must be probated after death, by a notary or by the court, at the cost of the succession. Quebec also holds a trap the other provinces do not. A marriage or civil union contract may contain a gift upon death in favour of a spouse, in a clause with the same legal force as a notarial will, and if that clause is stated to be irrevocable, changing it requires the spouses consent. A Quebecer updating a plan after a marriage, a separation or a new union should have the contract read alongside the will before assuming the will alone tells the whole story. A separate guide in this series will cover wills in Quebec on their own terms, including the notarial will, and Quebec readers should treat that guide and the Quebec sources below as their starting point.

## The calm takeaway

A will does not keep up with a life on its own. Review it every few years, and review it again at every marriage, birth, separation, death, move and major change in what you own. Know that your province may already have rewritten parts of it without telling you, in one direction in Manitoba and in another in Ontario and British Columbia, and that Quebec runs the whole question under its own Code. Then make your changes the proper way, in a codicil that meets the full formal test or in a fresh will that replaces the old one cleanly, and update the beneficiary forms that live outside the will at the same sitting. The document in the drawer should be a portrait of the family you have now, not the one you had when you signed it.
