For many young parents, this is the real reason a will moves from intention to paper. Money can be divided by a formula if it must be. Children cannot. Naming a guardian answers a human question, who would raise our children if we were both gone, and it deserves more care than a single hurried line. This guide explains what that naming does in law, what it cannot do, and how the main province rules differ. It is general legal information only, not legal advice for your own family. For your own situation, speak with a lawyer in your province, or a notary in Quebec.

This is one part of a complete will. ## What does naming a guardian in your will actually do?

In Manitoba, the provincial government puts the starting point simply: if a person has children under 18, their will should appoint a guardian to be responsible for the children care in the event both parents die, and the guardian may also be appointed to look after the children financial affairs. That sentence holds for parents across Canada in broad terms. A will is the place where the appointment belongs, and a godparent named in a family conversation, however loved, holds no legal role by that naming alone.

What the appointment does is speak for you at the moment you no longer can. It tells family, officials, and a court exactly who you chose. What it does not do, anywhere in Canada, is place the matter entirely beyond a court. The Government of Manitoba warns that naming a guardian does not guarantee that person will be chosen if there is a dispute among friends or relatives who want to care for the children. The naming gives the court a clear indication of the parent wishes and can carry substantial weight as the court decides. A court will always put the child interests first, and a considered choice by both parents is powerful evidence of what those interests are.

## Who should you choose, and what should you ask them?

Choose for the life your children would actually live. The right guardian knows your children and is known by them, shares the values you would want carried into daily decisions about schooling, health, and the shape of a home, and has the health, the time, and the stability to take on years of parenting, not only a difficult first month. Geography matters as well. A guardian across the country may be the right person and still mean a new province and a new school at the worst possible moment. Sometimes that move is worth it. It should at least be chosen with open eyes.

Then ask, before you write the name down. Manitoba guidance makes the same practical point it makes about executors: when naming someone as a guardian of children in a will, check and confirm the person is willing and able to assume the responsibilities involved. Consent is more than politeness. In Ontario, no appointment by will is effective without the consent of the person appointed, and in British Columbia an appointment does not take effect unless the appointed person accepts it, expressly or by their conduct. A conversation now is how that consent becomes real. Our guide to [choosing an executor](/guides/choosing-an-executor/) follows the same discipline for the other great naming in a will, and the two conversations often happen in the same season of life.

Name a backup guardian as well. Your first choice may be unable to act when the moment comes, through illness, a move, or a change in their own family. A backup costs one line in the will and spares a court from starting with a blank page.

## Both parents, the same names, and the surviving parent

Coordinate the two wills. If you and the other parent each make a will, name the same guardian and the same backup in both. Ontario law shows why precision matters. Where two or more people entitled to decision making responsibility die at the same time, or in circumstances that render it uncertain which survived the other, only the appointments of the persons appointed by both or all of them are effective. Two wills naming two different people can therefore cancel the plan out in exactly the situation the plan was built for.

Understand the limit that protects a surviving parent. A guardian appointment in a will is built for the loss of both parents, or for the loss of the only parent who held responsibility. Ontario law says an appointment takes effect only if the person who made it was the only person entitled to decision making responsibility on the day immediately before the appointment was to take effect, or if that person and every other person entitled died at the same time or in uncertain circumstances. If the other parent survives and holds responsibility, that parent ordinarily continues to raise the child, whatever your will says. Parents who are separated should take advice on their own facts, because orders and agreements about their children can change how an appointment works.

## How long does an appointment in a will last in Ontario?

Ontario parents should know one number, ninety days. Under section 61 of the Children Law Reform Act, an appointment by will for decision making responsibility or for guardianship of property expires ninety days after the appointment becomes effective. If the appointed person applies under the Act within that period of ninety days, the appointment continues until the application is decided. In plain terms, the will gives your chosen person immediate standing and a clear window, and the court then settles the longer arrangement. A guardian who knows this deadline in advance is far better placed than one who learns it late, which is one more reason to talk with the person you name and to leave them a note about where your will is kept.

British Columbia takes a different route. Under section 53 of the Family Law Act, a child guardian may appoint a person to be the child guardian on the death of the appointing guardian, either in a will or in the prescribed form signed in front of two witnesses, neither of whom is the person appointed. Section 55 adds a tool Ontario parents do not have in the same form: a guardian facing terminal illness or permanent mental incapacity may appoint a standby guardian, who steps in when the conditions in the appointment are met and who must consult with the appointing guardian to the fullest possible extent. In both kinds of appointment, the person named can receive no greater parental responsibilities than the appointing guardian had, the appointment must consider only the best interests of the child, and it takes effect only when the appointed person accepts it.

## What happens to the money a child inherits?

Keep two jobs separate in your mind, even if one trusted person eventually holds both. The guardian raises the child. The trustee holds and manages money left for the child, under the terms your will sets, paying out for care, health, and education and handing over what remains at an age you choose. A will that names a guardian and says nothing about money can leave a large share payable outright far earlier than most parents would wish, so the trust terms belong in the same conversation as the guardian name, as our guide to [what to put in a will](/guides/what-to-put-in-a-will/) explains.

Ontario also separates care of a child from care of a child property in its court system. The province explains that a child parent or caregiver is not automatically entitled to receive and manage the child money when the child is to receive money from an estate or another source. Generally the money is paid into court, held by the Accountant of the Superior Court of Justice, and paid to the child at the required age, usually 18. Where the total is 35,000 dollars or less, the Children Law Reform Act allows payment to a parent with whom the child resides or to a person who has lawful custody of the child. Above that level, a person who wants to receive and manage the money must be appointed guardian of property by the court, with a management plan the court approves. None of this replaces a trust in a will. It is the background system that applies when a will has not made its own arrangements.

A short letter of wishes, kept with your will, can help a guardian in ways a legal clause cannot. It is not binding, and it should never contradict the will, but it can record the schools you hope for, the family ties you want kept strong, and the reasons behind a choice that might surprise relatives. Clarity of this kind lowers the temperature of any later dispute, because the family can see a considered plan rather than a bare name.

## Quebec names a tutor, under its own rules

Quebec settles these questions under the Civil Code of Quebec, and the vocabulary changes because the institution changes. The person parents designate to care for a child and administer the child property after their death or incapacity is a dative tutor. Gouvernement du Québec explains that parents may designate one or more dative tutors in anticipation of their incapacity or death, and that they may do so in three places: in their protection mandate, in their will, whether notarized, handwritten, or drawn up before witnesses, or in a Declaration of Dative Tutorship sent to the Curateur public. A declaration filed with the Curateur public has official status and does not require further action by the court or a notary.

Three Quebec details deserve plain statement. First, in all cases the wishes of the last parent to die or to become incapable are the wishes that apply, so coordination between parents matters in Quebec as much as anywhere in Canada. Second, parents may designate a substitute dative tutor in the same document, who takes over if the designated person refuses the office, neglects their obligations, or dies. Third, timing runs from knowledge: a designated tutor has 30 days to accept or refuse the role, is presumed to have accepted unless they refuse within 30 days of learning of the appointment, and must communicate the decision to the Curateur public and, after a death, to the liquidator of the succession. Where parents have designated a tutor in advance, the court does not need to step in to appoint one, though the court will verify a will that is not notarized and will be involved in giving effect to a protection mandate.

Because the Quebec system is genuinely its own, Quebec parents should build from Quebec sources and Quebec advice from the start. ## Keeping the choice current

A guardian named for a toddler may be the wrong guardian for a teenager, and a guardian who was perfect at forty may be struggling at seventy. Review the appointment whenever you review the will itself, and in particular after a birth or adoption, a separation, a move to another province, the death or illness of a named guardian, or a major change in a child needs. If you have no will yet, our guide on [dying without a will in Canada](/guides/do-you-need-a-will-in-canada/) shows what the default rules do instead, and guardian choice is reason enough for most parents to move a will to the top of the list. ## The calm takeaway

Name the person who would raise your children, confirm they are willing and able, name the same person in both parents wills, add a backup, and separate the care of your children from the care of their money with a trust you choose on purpose. Know that a court keeps the final responsibility for a child welfare, and that your clear, considered, and current choice is the strongest guidance that court can receive. Written down while the choice is yours, it is one of the kindest lines in any will.
