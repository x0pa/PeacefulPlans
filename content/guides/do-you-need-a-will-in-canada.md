Almost everyone knows they should probably have a will. Far fewer people can say what actually happens without one, and that unknown is usually scarier than the reality. The reality is not chaos. It is a set of provincial rules, written in advance, that divide your estate for you. This guide explains what a will does, what it cannot do, and how those backup rules work, so you can decide for yourself whether the default plan is the plan you want.

## What does a will actually do?

A will is a legal document that speaks from the moment of your death. In it, you name an executor, the person or institution that gathers your assets, pays your debts and taxes, and distributes what remains. You say who receives your property, from the house and the bank accounts down to specific belongings that carry meaning far beyond their price. If you have young children, you can name a guardian for them and set up trusts that hold their share until an age you choose, rather than handing a large sum to an eighteen year old all at once.

A will also lets you do quieter, kinder things. You can leave a gift to a charity you care about. You can say who should take the family cottage, and on what terms. You can appoint someone to care for a pet. None of these wishes carries legal weight simply because your family knows about them. They carry weight because they are written in a valid will.

## What does a will not control?

A will governs more than most people expect, and less than many assume. Some assets pass outside the will entirely, according to their own paperwork. Life insurance with a named beneficiary is paid directly to that person. Registered accounts such as a TFSA or an RRSP with a named beneficiary usually pass the same way. Property held jointly with a right of survivorship, common between spouses, passes to the surviving owner automatically.

Pension survivor benefits follow the rules of the pension plan and the law behind it, not your will. This is why a will works best as one part of a small set of papers that agree with each other. When you make or update a will, it is worth glancing at the beneficiary forms on your accounts and insurance, and making sure the two tell the same story.

## If there is no will, intestacy steps in

Dying without a will is called dying intestate, and every province and territory has an intestacy law that answers three questions in a fixed order. Who is entitled to share in the estate? In what proportions? And who may apply to the court to administer it all?

The general shape is similar across the common law provinces. A surviving spouse stands first in line. If there are children as well, the estate is divided between the spouse and the children under a formula set by that province. If there is no spouse and no children, the line moves outward to parents, then brothers and sisters, then nieces and nephews, and further if needed. If no relatives can be found at all, the estate may eventually pass to the province itself.

Two consequences surprise people. First, the formula cannot bend. It makes no room for the friend who cared for you daily, the stepchild you raised as your own, or the charity you supported for decades, unless they fall inside the legal categories. In most provinces, a common law partner is treated very differently from a married spouse under intestacy, and in some provinces a common law partner has no automatic share at all. Second, someone still has to do the executor's work. A family member must usually apply to the court for the authority to act, which can mean delay and disagreement at exactly the moment a family can least use either.

## The provincial differences are real

Wills and estates are provincial law in Canada, and the details genuinely vary. The amount a spouse receives before children share, sometimes called a preferential share, is set by each province and the figures differ widely. The rules for common law partners differ. The age at which a child receives their share outright, and how it is held until then, differ as well.

Manitoba offers a clear example of a province that has thought about this in its own way. Under Manitoba law, if a person dies without a will leaving a spouse or common law partner and no children, the partner generally takes the whole estate. Where children are involved, Manitoba divides the estate under its own formula, and Manitoba law has long treated common law partners who meet its conditions much more like married spouses than some other provinces do. The lesson is not the Manitoba detail itself. The lesson is that your province has a specific answer, and it may not match what your neighbour in the next province assumes.

Quebec stands apart, as it does in most legal matters. Intestacy there follows the Civil Code of Quebec, with its own order of relatives and its own shares, and the person who settles the estate is called a liquidator rather than an executor. Nothing in this guide's general description should be stretched over Quebec without checking the Quebec rules directly.

## So, do you need one?

Strictly speaking, nobody is required to make a will. The province will settle your estate without one. The honest question is whether the provincial formula happens to match your wishes. For a person whose whole estate would go to a spouse anyway, and who is content with the default, a will still earns its keep by naming an executor and a guardian rather than leaving both to a court application.

For almost everyone else, the formula and your wishes will part company somewhere. A blended family, a common law relationship, a child with a disability, a business, a cottage, a wish to remember a friend or a charity, a wish to treat children unequally for good and loving reasons: each of these is a place where the default rules cannot follow you. A will can.

## The calm takeaway

You do not need a will because disaster is coming. You need one because your province already has a plan for your estate, and it was written for nobody in particular. A will simply replaces that general plan with your own, in your own words, while you are here to write it.
